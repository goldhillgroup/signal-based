3Running a search

Signal Radar → the form is already filled in with sensible defaults, so you can often just press Search.

  1. Mode. Leave it on Hybrid. That looks for founder-and-successor pairs and keeps every other good family business it passes on the way.
  2. Verticals. Eight are available — landscaping, home building, construction, specialty trades, manufacturing, distribution, property services, professional services. Pick any combination, or none to search all eight.
  3. States. Your twelve are pre-selected: California, Florida, Texas, New York and the Northeast.
  4. Revenue band. Defaults to $5–15M, the sweet spot. $5–30M widens it to your full profile.
  5. How many to find. Higher numbers read more companies and take longer. The form tells you how many it will read and roughly what it will cost.
  6. Signal focus (optional). Tick any of the twelve signals from your profile, or type your own words. This changes what the search looks for.
  7. Press Search. It shows exactly what it will read and what it will cost, and waits for you to confirm.

A big search runs in several passes. The server stops any single run after five minutes. Everything found is saved and the next pass starts on its own — you do not have to do anything.

Searching the same thing twice is not wasted. Every company already looked at is remembered and skipped, so a repeat search goes deeper into the same ground rather than handing back the same list.

4Reading the results

Each search creates a folder. Inside, leads are split three ways.

Founder + successor

Both generations named, both running it today. This is what the product is for — and the rarest, about one company in thirty.

Good fit, no successor yet

Right trade, right area, family-run — but nobody named to take over. Still worth a call.

Not a fit

Cut, with the reason on the row. Shown so you can disagree with it.

The quote is the point

Every confirmed pair carries a sentence from the company's own website, with a link to the page it came from. You can check any claim in one click. If a lead has no quote, it has not earned the label — and the system lowers it rather than pretending.

5Finding email addresses

Separate from searching, and never automatic — it costs money per address found.

  1. Press Find emails — on the Overview, inside a folder, or on the Enrichment screen. All three do the same thing.
  2. Tick who you want: founder + successor, good fits, or cut but arguable. Pairs and fits are ticked for you.
  3. The button shows the number of companies and the most it can cost. Press it to start.

You are only charged when an address is actually found. A miss costs nothing, so the real figure is almost always lower than the estimate. Every address found is then checked to make sure it is deliverable.

It looks for the successor first. Where a next-generation family member is named, that is who it tries to reach — the founder is the fallback.

7Settings

One thing lives here: the six vendor keys, each with its balance shown live beside it. If a service ever runs out, this is where you paste a new key, and it takes effect immediately — no developer, no waiting.

Everything about what a search looks for is on the search form itself — the verticals, the states, the revenue band and the signal focus. There is no second place to set them, so nothing can be quietly steering a search from a screen you have not opened.

10Questions you are likely to have

How many leads should I expect?

A search reads twenty to sixty companies and typically keeps five to ten. Of those, a confirmed founder-and-successor pair turns up about once in every thirty companies read — so a single search often finds none, and a few searches find several. The good family-owned companies without a named successor are kept either way, because they are still worth a call.

Why does it reject so many?

Because searching the web for "family landscaping business" also returns funeral homes, trade magazines, associations and software companies. Those are removed silently. What you see under "Not a fit" is only the arguable ones — right sort of company, failed one of your tests — so you can disagree with it.

Can I trust what it says about a company?

Every confirmed pair carries a sentence from the company's own website and a link to the page. If the quote does not say what the lead claims, that is a fault worth reporting. Nothing is included on the strength of a shared surname or a guess.

Do I have to run it often?

No, and nothing runs on its own at all — there is no scheduled job and no background activity. Every cost follows a button you pressed. Leave it a month and it costs you the $29 Apify subscription and nothing else.

What if I search the same thing twice?

It goes further rather than repeating itself. Every company already looked at is remembered and skipped, so a second search of the same trade and state finds new companies. Your existing leads stay exactly where they are.

Can I change what it looks for?

Yes, in Settings — the revenue band and the signal focus. Change them once and every future search follows. You can also set them per search on the form.

Something is wrong with a lead. What do I do?

Open it, read the quote, and check the source page. If the page supports it and you still disagree, that is a judgement call worth telling Daniel about — the rules that decide a lead were built from your own list of accepted and rejected companies, and they can be adjusted the same way.
